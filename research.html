Deadpooling: Strategic Lock-In in the Medigap Market

with Elaine Shen

Click here for latest version

Abstract

The Medigap market prevents adverse selection by locking enrollees in to their plan; after an initial enrollment window, insurers may reject applicants who fail medical underwriting, so most enrollees have only one opportunity to shop. We show that insurers profit from this lock-in through corporate structure. Linking two decades of Medigap filings to parent–subsidiary ownership records, we document deadpooling. In this practice, one subsidiary closes a plan to new enrollees, a sister subsidiary opens a replacement for healthy applicants, and premiums rise on the enrollees left behind. From 2006–2020, 17.6% of Medigap life-years are spent in plans that deadpool. Using a Callaway–Sant’Anna staggered difference-in-differences design, we find that an enrollee who stays in their plan pays $762 more in premiums five years after closure than if their plan had not closed. Each year’s closures transfer $64.7 million from seniors to insurer profits and cost seniors $108.3 million in lost consumer surplus, including switching costs and lost coverage. Overall, seniors lose $1.67 in consumer surplus per dollar of insurer gain. With variation from the staggered adoption of switching laws in six states, we find that allowing enrollees to switch plans annually reduces enrollment in deadpooling plans by 18%. A calibrated model shows that policies that do not rely on consumer attention may better improve market outcomes.

The Price of “Free” Advice: Measuring Agency Problems in the Medicare Broker Market

with Elaine Shen

Click here for latest version

Abstract

Consumers turn to experts because they cannot evaluate complex choices, yet these same frictions can leave them unable to evaluate the experts themselves. Agency problems can therefore persist even in competitive, highly regulated markets. We study this problem in the Medicare insurance broker market, a market serving almost 70 million seniors and individuals with disabilities. We conduct an audit study where we hire senior “secret shoppers” to make 637 calls to randomly assigned brokers. Brokers provide at least one misaligned recommendation on 52% of calls and dominated recommendations can cost seniors over $12,000 in present-value lifetime wealth. A natural experiment eliminating broker commissions reduces steering towards a higher-commission plan by 21p.p., but does not eliminate it. A treatment variation designed to increase perceived competition by signaling that seniors will collect multiple recommendations causes brokers to recommend lower-priced plans. Competing on price, however, may also lower plan quality and consumer surplus. Using a structural model to estimate counterfactual policies, we find that supply-side reforms such as equalizing commissions and directing seniors to more experienced brokers lead to larger gains in consumer surplus than policies that can be directly implemented by seniors themselves (e.g. consumer feedback and competitive pressure). The findings highlight limitations to using consumer choice and competition to discipline markets for complex products.

The Gap in Medigap: Disenrollment from Medicare Advantage Among Disabled Beneficiaries at Age 65

with Daria Pelech

Abstract

Medicare provides essential coverage for roughly 7 million disabled individuals, but fee-for-service Medicare’s cost sharing can expose beneficiaries to substantial financial risk. Medigap plans help cover these costs, yet access for beneficiaries under age 65 varies widely among states. Some states don’t require insurers to offer Medigap coverage to younger beneficiaries; others permit substantially higher premiums.

We combined Medigap premium data with beneficiary-level enrollment records to examine how Medigap policies affect coverage decisions. At age 65, all Medicare beneficiaries have guaranteed issue rights in Medigap; insurers must offer Medigap coverage without health-based underwriting. Using this policy change and variation in state regulations, we tested whether disabled beneficiaries disenrolled from Medicare Advantage (MA), Medigap’s primary substitute.

Among disabled beneficiaries enrolled in MA for at least one year, disenrollment at age 65 was roughly three times the baseline rate and varied substantially by state policy. Disenrollment increased most in states with limited Medigap access before age 65 and was lower where regulations expanded access and affordability. These findings suggest that Medigap policies significantly influence coverage choices among disabled Medicare beneficiaries.
